ChordScribe

I wanted to, in a day, build a Mac app that could listen to any song and write out its chords. Yes, there are websites a plenty and YouTube videos out there, but I wanted to combine my hobbies of guitar playing with seeing what Claude Code could do. So I built one, then tested it against official tabs to find out where it worked and where it tapped out.

Allowed is the share of time showing a chord root the tab lists for that section. Found is the share of a section's listed roots the app actually showed. Keep in mind these are section-level checks, not chord-by-chord accuracy.

What it does

You drop in a song file. About 30 seconds later, the chords scroll in time with the music, with the current and next chord, a guitar shape for each one, and a list of the song's main chords. Everything runs locally on the Mac, so no audio is uploaded.

  1. Separates the sustained, pitched sound from the drums.
  2. Finds the beat and measures which of the 12 notes sound on each beat.
  3. Compares each beat with 48 chord templates: major, minor, 7 and minor 7 on every root.
  4. Chooses the most likely sequence of chords, so one noisy beat can't flip the answer.

On audio I generated with known chords, it reached 97.0% for root and major/minor. That proved the pipeline worked. It said very little about real recordings.

How it did on real songs

The test set is eight songs from my local library that have official Ultimate-Guitar.com tabs. Four were for tuning. Four were held back and scored one time, after the app was frozen. A ninth song is a best-case showcase, picked because it suits the app, so it isn't evidence of typical accuracy.

Not surprisingly, Nick Cave's tender and restrained "Where the Wild Roses Grow" performed best.

What failed

Unfortunately, the hardest problem was distorted power chords, the core of the music I listen to. Every fix below looked reasonable. Testing is what stopped the bad ones from shipping.

What I learned

  • The root often isn't the loudest low note. Obviously and a big "duh" given the layers of guitars, bass and the overall mix. In a Helmet D5 passage, the strongest low pitches included A2 and D3, the chord's fifth and upper root, with no D2 near the top [Shakes fist at Page Hamilton]. In a Pantera passage, B2 and E3 led for an E5. Single-note rules keep missing what a listener hears from the whole sound.
  • A good average can hide a bad trade. One change improved power chords while wrecking sevenths, and only the song-by-song rows showed it.
  • A score needs a baseline. My first metric gave 100% to a program that repeats D forever, so I added Found and an always-one-chord comparison.
  • Test on songs you didn't tune on. The change that scored 87.4% on one song fell apart on the next. Holding songs back caught that before it reached the app.
  • The first reference was bad. A user-made chord chart barely fit the recording at any pitch, so I dropped it for official tabs.

What I'd try next

Hand-written rules have probably reached their limit here, there's just too many variables to cover. The next test would be a trained chord model, judged on the same songs. New held-back songs would be needed, because these four have now been used. Live listening and draft tabs would come after that, since both depend on reading the chords correctly first.

Built with Claude Code. I set the goals, made the product decisions and judged results by ear as a guitarist, and Claude wrote the code. Python, librosa and a local web player, on an old Intel Mac. Scored November 2026. No audio, lyrics or tab text are included here.
