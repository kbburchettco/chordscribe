ChordCruncher

I wanted to, in a day, build a Mac app that could listen to any song and quickly write out its chords. Yes, there are websites aplenty and YouTube videos out there with tabs and chords, but I wanted to see how quickly a Claude Code-built app could handle it. So I built it, then tested it against official tabs to find out where it worked and where it tapped out.

Tab match is how much of the song showed a chord the tab lists for that part. An 88% tab match means the app agreed with the tab about 88% of the time. Chords caught is how many of the chords listed for each part actually appeared. These are estimates by song section, not exact chord-by-chord accuracy.

What it does

You drop in a song file. About 30 seconds later, the chords scroll in time with the music, with the current and next chord, a guitar shape for each one, and a list of the song's main chords. Everything runs locally on the Mac, so no audio is uploaded.

  1. Separates the sustained, pitched sound from the drums.
  2. Finds the beat and measures which of the 12 notes sound on each beat.
  3. Compares each beat with 48 chord templates: major, minor, 7 and minor 7 on every root.
  4. Chooses the most likely sequence of chords, so one noisy beat can't flip the answer.

On audio I generated with known chords, it reached 97.0% for root and major/minor. That proved the pipeline worked. It said very little about real recordings.

How it did on real songs

I tested it in two rounds, using songs with official Ultimate-Guitar.com tabs. The first round leaned on heavy, riff-based songs, and the app struggled. Four songs held back from all adjustments averaged a 66.2% tab match and 54.2% chords caught.

So I ran a second round with six rock and pop songs the app had never heard, without changing how it detects chords. Those songs averaged an 82.5% tab match and 75.4% chords caught.

Not surprisingly, Nick Cave's tender and restrained "Where the Wild Roses Grow" had the highest tab match, at 97.6%. Echo & the Bunnymen's "The Killing Moon" caught the most chords, at 97.0%.

Each pair shows tab match / chords caught. "Always one chord" is the control: the best score from showing the same chord for the whole song. It can score well on tab match, but it can't catch many chords, so chords caught is the better sign of real listening. Song counted is how much of the song could be compared with the tab.

These scores count every section that lists chords. That change came after the first results, when a stricter rule counted as little as 17% of a song. Under that rule, the six new songs averaged 80.9% / 77.3%. Section times are approximate, because tabs run at a steady tempo and real bands drift. The Breathe tab lists no verse chords and its intro chords look doubtful, so its score may say more about the tab than the app. Pennywise's Bro Hymn recording is a longer version than its tab, and every section lists the same chords.

What failed

Unfortunately, the hardest problem was distorted power chords, the core of the music I listen to. Every fix below looked reasonable. Testing is what stopped the bad ones from shipping.

What I learned

  • The root often isn't the loudest low note. Obviously and a big "duh" given the layers of guitars, bass and the overall mix. In a Helmet D5 passage, the strongest low pitches included A2 and D3, the chord's fifth and upper root, with no D2 near the top [Shakes fist at Page Hamilton]. In a Pantera passage, B2 and E3 led for an E5. Single-note rules keep missing what a listener hears from the whole sound.
  • A good average can hide a bad trade. One change improved power chords while wrecking sevenths, and only the song-by-song rows showed it.
  • A score needs a baseline. My first metric gave 100% to a program that repeats D forever, so I added Chords caught and an always-one-chord comparison.
  • Skipping the hard parts flatters a score. My first scoring skipped busy sections, which left only 17% of Behind Blue Eyes counted and gave Wild Roses a perfect 100% for chords caught. That looked wrong to me. Counting those sections gave a fairer 80.2%.
  • Test on songs the app hasn't seen. The change that scored 87.4% on one song fell apart on the next. Holding songs back caught that before it reached the app.
  • The first reference was bad. A user-made chord chart barely fit the recording at any pitch, so I dropped it instead for official Ultimate-Guitar tabs.

What I'd try next

Hand-written rules have probably reached their limit here, there are just too many variables to cover. The next test would be a trained chord model, judged on the same songs. New held-back songs would be needed, because these have now been used. Live listening and draft tabs would come after that, since both depend on reading the chords correctly first. The human ear remains undefeated...for how long we'll have to see.
